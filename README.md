CS 255 – System Analysis and Design

DriverPass System Analysis and Design Project

Project Overview

This repository includes my work from CS 255: System Analysis and Design at Southern New Hampshire University. It contains two primary artifacts: the Business Requirements Document from Project One and the System Design Document from Project Two.

These projects demonstrate my ability to gather business requirements, analyze client needs, develop system models, and design software solutions that address real-world challenges.

1. Briefly summarize the DriverPass project. Who was the client? What type of system did they want you to design?

The client, DriverPass, helps students prepare for driving exams through online practice tests and behind-the-wheel lessons. They required a system for students to register, purchase training packages, schedule lessons, and track progress. The system also needed to support employees in managing appointments, driver availability, vehicles, and customer information.

I analyzed the client's business needs and designed a system to improve organization and efficiency. This included creating a business requirements document and UML diagrams to illustrate system functionality. The goal was to develop a user-friendly system for students that also met DriverPass employees' operational needs.

2. What did you do particularly well?

I excelled at breaking down the client's requirements and identifying how different users would interact with the system. Instead of treating DriverPass as a single application, I focused on each user group's specific responsibilities, including students, secretaries, drivers, the owner, and the IT officer.

I also effectively connected business requirements to system design. My IT experience helped me consider not only what the system should do, but also how it would be used. For example, when designing scheduling functionality, I accounted for driver and vehicle availability rather than allowing students to select any appointment time.

This project enhanced my ability to think critically about how system components work together to support business operations.

3. If you could choose one part of your work on these documents to revise, what would you pick? How would you improve it?

If I were to revise one aspect of my work, I would improve the UML diagrams in my system design document, especially the use case and sequence diagrams.

While my diagrams covered the main functions of the DriverPass system, I could have clarified the relationships and interactions between users and system components. As I added more actors and functions, some diagrams became more complex than anticipated.

I would enhance the diagrams by organizing information more effectively, reviewing each connection for accuracy, and ensuring they are accessible to both technical and nontechnical audiences. I would also add more alternative scenarios, such as when a student tries to schedule a lesson, but no driver or vehicle is available.

I learned that creating a diagram isn't just about including all information; it's about communicating it clearly to those who will use it.

4. How did you interpret the user's needs and implement them into your system design? Why is it so important to consider the user's needs when designing?

I interpreted user needs by reviewing the DriverPass interview transcript and identifying the specific functions and expectations discussed. I then translated these business needs into functional and nonfunctional requirements to guide system design.

For example, students needed a convenient way to schedule lessons, so I included scheduling features that consider available drivers, vehicles, and training packages. To help employees manage appointments and customer information, I added administrative functions and role-based access.

Understanding user needs is essential in software development. A system may be technically advanced, but if it doesn't address the user's actual problem, it won't succeed.

My IT experience has shown me that users often do not express their needs in technical terms. They may describe a problem or frustration, and we are responsible for understanding their goals. Listening, asking questions, and clarifying expectations can prevent unnecessary changes and costly mistakes later in development.

5. How do you approach designing software? What techniques or strategies would you use in the future to analyze and design a system?

My approach to software design begins with understanding the problem before developing a solution. It is important to define business goals, identify users, and determine system objectives.

Next, I gather and organize requirements, identify potential challenges, and use modeling techniques to visualize how the system operates. I use UML use case diagrams for user interactions, activity diagrams for workflows, sequence diagrams for system communication, and class diagrams for system structure.

In future projects, I plan to incorporate more user feedback throughout the design process. Regular communication with stakeholders and ongoing requirements reviews can help identify issues early and ensure the final product meets expectations.

I will also continue breaking larger problems into smaller, manageable components. This approach makes complex systems easier to understand, design, test, and maintain.

Final Reflection

CS 255 has deepened my understanding of the planning and design work that precedes software development. With an IT background, I was familiar with troubleshooting and supporting daily operations, but this course provided a new perspective by emphasizing system analysis, modeling, and design before implementation.

A key takeaway is that successful software development requires more than technical expertise. It also demands communication, organization, critical thinking, and an understanding of diverse user needs.

As I pursue my bachelor's degree in Computer Science with a concentration in Software Engineering, I plan to apply these skills to future projects and my career. My goal is to become a software engineer who develops functional applications and delivers practical, efficient, and meaningful solutions for users.
